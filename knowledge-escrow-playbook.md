# Knowledge Escrow
### The FDE Handover Interview Playbook

> In an acquisition, escrow protects the buyer by holding money back until risk clears.
> **Knowledge Escrow** does the same for what's in people's heads: capture it, verify it and structure it before the people who hold it walk out the door.

---

## Objective

These interviews extract:

- undocumented operating knowledge
- exceptions to written processes
- customer and vendor relationships
- pricing and commercial judgment
- key-person dependencies
- company vocabulary and aliases
- historical context
- decision rules
- workarounds
- risks that disappear when someone leaves
- information that contradicts or clarifies system data

**The goal is not to finish a checklist.** It's to get the person talking, follow the important threads, and turn their knowledge into structured company memory.

---

## Interview craft

**It's a conversation, not a deposition.**

- **Open human.** Coffee, weekend plans, how long they've been here, the dog in the photo on their desk. Five minutes of rapport buys an hour of candour.
- **Let it wander, a little.** Tangents (a childhood story, a favourite snack, a fishing trip, a rival sports team) are fine and often lead somewhere useful ("...which is how I ended up running the Kentucky shop"). Steer back gently.
- **Laugh with them.** People reveal more when they're comfortable. Self-deprecating humour from the interviewer helps.
- **Never correct them in the moment.** Write it down and verify against records later. "Interesting, I'll double-check that" keeps them talking.
- **Chase the vocabulary.** When they use a nickname, acronym or shorthand ("the Kentucky shop", "a hot one", "the old system"), ask what it means. These are aliases and jargon your ontology needs.
- **Ask for stories, not summaries.** "Tell me about the last time that happened" beats "how often does that happen".
- **Record certainty.** For every number or date, ask: "Is that exact, or your best guess? Where would I check?"
- **Respect sensitivity.** Pay, discipline, health and deal terms come up. Note them as restricted and don't repeat them to others.
- **Close with the magic questions.** "What should I have asked that I didn't?" and "What breaks if we don't capture this?"

### Preferred openers

- Tell me about...
- Walk me through...
- What happens when...
- How do you decide...
- Can you give me an example...
- What usually goes wrong...
- Who would know...
- What would I misunderstand if...
- What isn't written down...
- What changed, and why...

Avoid questions that can be answered with yes or no.

### Threads worth following

Whenever the interviewee mentions any of these, consider following the thread:

- a person, customer, vendor or asset
- a process, exception, workaround or rule
- a nickname or acronym
- a number
- a historical event
- a disagreement
- a risk

# PART 1: Seller / Founder Interview

## 1. The story of the business

1. Tell me the story of how this business started.
2. What did the company originally do, and how has that changed?
3. Who were your first important customers?
4. What were the major turning points in the company's history?
5. Were there periods where the company nearly failed?
6. What decisions made the biggest difference?
7. Which acquisitions, expansions, new locations or major hires changed the business?
8. What about the company today only makes sense if you know the history?

**Follow-ups:** Why? What happened next? Who was involved? Is that written down anywhere? Does anyone else know that story? Does that history still affect how you operate today?

## 2. How the business actually makes money

1. Walk me through how the company makes money.
2. What are the major service or product lines?
3. Which are most profitable?
4. Which look profitable but actually aren't?
5. What work do you love getting?
6. What work do you try to avoid?
7. Are there services you provide mainly to keep another relationship?
8. Where does margin usually get lost?
9. How does the company accidentally leave money on the table?
10. If you had to improve EBITDA by 10% next year, where would you look first?

## 3. Customer acquisition

1. Walk me through how a new customer typically becomes a customer.
2. Where do leads come from?
3. How long does the sales process take?
4. Who is involved?
5. Why does someone choose you over a competitor?
6. How do you decide an opportunity is worth pursuing?
7. Which customers are easiest to win?
8. Which are hardest?
9. How does bidding or quoting work?
10. Is there a pricing calculator, spreadsheet, rate book, template or rule of thumb?
11. Which parts of quoting depend on judgment rather than a formula?
12. What do you need to know before you're comfortable giving a price?

## 4. Pricing

1. Walk me through how you decide what to charge.
2. What are the standard rates?
3. When do you deviate from them?
4. Who is allowed to approve discounts?
5. Are there customers with special pricing that isn't obvious from the system?
6. Are there customers you deliberately charge less? Why?
7. Are there customers you charge more because they're difficult, risky, remote or urgent?
8. What happens when costs increase?
9. How do price increases get communicated?
10. Has the written pricing policy ever differed from what actually happens?
11. Which pricing decisions would be hard for someone else to make without you?

## 5. Customers

1. Who are the most important customers, and why?
2. Which customers would worry you most if they left?
3. Which relationships depend heavily on you personally?
4. Who are the real decision-makers at those customers?
5. Are there customers where the person in the CRM isn't really the important person?
6. Who is difficult to deal with?
7. Who always pays late?
8. Who has unusual billing requirements?
9. Who has unusual site requirements?
10. Who gets exceptions?
11. Which customers have unwritten expectations?
12. Which customers have history a new owner wouldn't understand?
13. Where do you tolerate bad economics for another reason?
14. Which relationships should the buyer personally take over before you leave?

**For each major customer:**
- What do they call us for?
- Who do they trust here, and who do we trust there?
- What makes them unhappy?
- What exceptions do they get?
- Which contract terms actually matter?
- What's the history?
- What could cause us to lose them?

## 6. Vendors and suppliers

1. Who are the critical vendors?
2. Which vendors could seriously hurt operations if they disappeared?
3. Who gives you special pricing?
4. Which relationships are personal rather than institutional?
5. Are there suppliers people still use even though they shouldn't?
6. Are there unofficial backup suppliers?
7. Are there hard-to-source parts or materials?
8. Who knows how to source unusual items?
9. Which vendor relationships live mostly in phone calls and texts rather than in the official system?
10. Are there suppliers you'd warn a new owner about?

## 7. End-to-end operations

> **Walk me through a normal job from the moment the customer calls until the company gets paid.**

Then follow every step:

```text
Customer request → triage → scheduling → technician assignment → site access → diagnosis
→ parts → repair → work-order closeout → customer approval → invoicing → collections
```

At every step, ask:

1. Who owns this?
2. What system do they use?
3. What information do they need, and where does it come from?
4. What commonly goes wrong?
5. What are the exceptions?
6. What do experienced people know that newer ones don't?
7. Is there documentation?
8. Is the documented process how people actually work?

## 8. Emergencies and exceptions

1. What happens when something goes wrong at 2 AM?
2. Who gets called first?
3. How do you decide which employee to send?
4. Are there customers that always jump the queue?
5. Who can override the normal process?
6. What emergencies happen repeatedly?
7. Which customers or machines behave differently from everything else?
8. What unusual problems can only one or two people solve?
9. What undocumented workarounds do people use?
10. When someone says "we always do it this way," what are they probably talking about?

## 9. People

1. Who can this company not operate without? Why?
2. What does each of them know that others don't?
3. Who is best at diagnosing unusual problems?
4. Who understands particular customers?
5. Who understands pricing?
6. Who knows where everything is?
7. Who actually runs operations, regardless of title?
8. Whose official role differs from what they really do?
9. Who should I spend the most time interviewing?
10. If five people resigned tomorrow, which five would scare you most?
11. Who is approaching retirement?
12. Who might leave after the acquisition?
13. Who could eventually replace you?

## 10. Company language

1. What terms or acronyms would confuse an outsider?
2. What nicknames do you use for customers?
3. What nicknames do you use for equipment?
4. What do people call the different locations?
5. Are there two people with similar names who get confused?
6. Do old company names still appear in files?
7. How does terminology differ between accounting, sales, operations and the field?
8. Which phrases would I need to follow a normal conversation here?

## 11. Systems and data

1. What software does the company use?
2. What did you use before?
3. What never made it into the new system?
4. Which system do you trust most, and which least?
5. Where do people keep their own spreadsheets?
6. Who has important information on their laptop?
7. Are there paper files that still matter?
8. What lives in email, and what lives mostly in text messages?
9. Where does the system say one thing while reality is different?
10. What data would you never trust without asking someone?

## 12. Policies vs reality

1. Which written policies aren't actually followed?
2. Are there unofficial policies everyone knows?
3. What exceptions have you personally authorized?
4. Are old policies lying around that someone might mistake for current?
5. Which rules changed recently?
6. Who still operates by the old rule?
7. What does the system say is true that isn't?
8. What requires judgment instead of following the written policy?

## 13. History and temporal truth

1. What important things changed in the last two years?
2. Which customers changed ownership or names?
3. Which employees changed roles?
4. Which pricing rules changed?
5. Which vendors changed?
6. Which operating processes changed?
7. Which old documents would be dangerous to treat as current?
8. Which decisions made sense at the time but don't any more?

## 14. Risks

1. What are you most worried about after you leave?
2. What could stop the company from operating?
3. What could cause a major customer to leave?
4. Which regulatory or licensing requirements depend on a particular person?
5. Which processes have no backup owner?
6. What knowledge has never been documented?
7. What would surprise the buyer six months from now?
8. What did diligence miss?
9. What problem have you been meaning to fix for years?
10. If I could capture only five pieces of knowledge from you before you leave, what should they be?

## 15. The "only you know" questions (always ask)

1. What do you know about this company that nobody else fully knows?
2. What do people constantly come to you to ask?
3. What decisions still require your judgment?
4. What relationships exist mainly because of you?
5. What information exists only in your memory?
6. What shortcuts, tricks or rules of thumb have you learned over the years?
7. What does the written documentation get wrong?
8. What should I ask you that I haven't asked?
9. What would you want your replacement to know before you walk out the door?
10. What will break if we don't capture it in the next 90 days?

# PART 2: Key Employee Interview

This interview focuses on how work actually happens.

## Opening
1. Tell me what you actually do here, not just your job title.
2. Walk me through a normal day.
3. What kinds of problems end up on your desk?
4. Who do you work with most?
5. What do people come to you for help with?

## Their process
1. Walk me through your most important process from beginning to end.
2. What information do you need, and where do you get it?
3. What tools or systems do you use?
4. Which steps aren't written down?
5. What shortcuts do experienced people know?
6. What mistakes do newer employees make?
7. What happens when the normal process doesn't work?

## Their knowledge
1. What do you know that would be hard to replace if you left?
2. Can anyone else do everything you do?
3. Who taught you?
4. Do you keep notes, spreadsheets, folders, texts or photos that help you?
5. What do you just remember rather than look up?
6. What customer or equipment quirks do you know?
7. Which rules do you ignore because they don't work in practice?

## Other people
1. Who knows the most about this company?
2. Who should I definitely interview?
3. Who actually knows how this process works?
4. Who knows the customers best?
5. Who has knowledge that isn't documented?
6. Whose departure tomorrow would cause the biggest problem?

## Contradictions
1. Is there anything you and the owner disagree about?
2. Are there reports or systems you don't trust?
3. What does management think happens that doesn't?
4. Which procedures look good on paper but don't work?
5. Which numbers do you think are misleading?

## Closing
1. What haven't I asked that I should understand?
2. What do outsiders usually misunderstand about this business?
3. What would you tell someone doing your job for the first time tomorrow?
4. What should we capture before the transition is over?

# The follow-up algorithm

Don't ask every question mechanically. Start broad, then:

```text
Ask a broad question
↓
Listen for entities, processes, exceptions, decisions and uncertainty
↓
Pick the most valuable thread
↓
Ask for an example or story
↓
Ask how the decision gets made
↓
Ask who else knows
↓
Ask where the evidence lives
↓
Ask whether it changed over time
↓
Ask about exceptions
↓
Ask what happens without that knowledge
```

### Follow-up triggers

| They mention... | Ask... |
|---|---|
| **A person** | What do they own? Why are they important? Can anyone else do that? Should I interview them? |
| **A customer** | What's special about them? Who owns the relationship? What exceptions do they get? Has that changed? |
| **An exception** | Why does it exist? Who approved it? Is it documented? Who else knows? Does the system reflect it? |
| **A process** | Walk me through it. What happens before and after? What goes wrong? Who decides? What isn't documented? |
| **A number** | Where does it come from? Exact or estimate? Is there a report that confirms it? |
| **Uncertainty** | Who would know? Where could we verify it? Is this something you believe or something you know? |
| **History** | When did it change? What was true before? Why did it change? Are old documents still around? |
| **Jargon or a nickname** | What does that mean? Who else uses that term? Is it the same thing as \[official name\]? |

# Capture requirements

For every important claim, capture:

```text
Claim
Speaker
Timestamp (in the recording)
Entities mentioned
Speaker's certainty: knows / estimates / believes / unsure
Effective date
Possible supporting source
Possible contradiction
Follow-up required
Sensitivity
```

**Interviews are evidence, not canonical truth.** Reconcile them against:
- contracts and policies
- system records
- email
- other interviews
- historical context

Interviews are most valuable for explaining **why**, finding unknowns, discovering relationships, and surfacing knowledge that was never documented.

# What a good exchange sounds like

```text
FDE:   Walk me through what happens when a customer calls with an emergency.
Dispatcher:  Depends who it is. If it's "the big one", I already know who I'm sending.
FDE:   When you say "the big one", who do you mean?
Dispatcher:  Oh, sorry, [Customer]. Everybody calls them that. Been our biggest forever.
FDE:   You said you already know who you're sending. How do you decide?
Dispatcher:  Usually [Senior Tech], unless it's refrigeration, then it's [Specialist].
FDE:   Is that rule written down anywhere?
Dispatcher:  [laughs] Honey, it's written down up here.
FDE:   Fair. Is that exact, or does it change?
Dispatcher:  It changes when someone's on vacation. Which, speaking of, I'm
             going to my sister's lake place in two weeks and I am NOT answering
             this phone.
FDE:   Noted. Who covers for you?
```

**The objective isn't just to collect facts. It's to reconstruct how the company actually thinks and operates.**
